In chapter 2 of his book, Pippin explores the notion of variations, and how they can be used to explore a single idea from many angles. That "what if?" question can completely alter the experience and meaning of a game. For example, Ghost Pong becomes an exercise in futility. Your actions have very little influence on the world around you. Is this all it is to haunt? To just move around? As Pippin says "the constant reinforcement of missing time and again, or rather of being passed through, starts to feel personal." It is a small change, but if you take most any idea seriously you can find lots to reflect on. Pippin goes through a few of his Pongs, noting lessons and ideas he found in each. One of the most important lessons in working with the structure of games is understanding how many of the most interesting results come from games' inherent emergent properties. If given the opportunity, players will happily alter the structure of a game and find entirely new possibilities than the designer even intended. Ultimately, as a live, reactive and interactive medium the rules of games can be as fluid or rigid as we want. Artists and designers understand that rules are a material, moldable and expressive. Learning to work with it is an important part of considered/conscious game design. These variations are one way of conversing with this material, of playing with it and learning its properties.
